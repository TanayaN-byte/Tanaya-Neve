India’s ₹500 Note Has Taken Over the Cash Mix

The value of ₹500 notes in circulation has risen sharply since demonetisation, while digital payments have changed how smaller-value transactions are made.

By Tanaya Neve · September 2026

In 2016, India’s currency system underwent its biggest change in decades when the government withdrew the old ₹500 and ₹1,000 notes from legal tender.

The move changed the composition of cash in circulation. The ₹1,000 note disappeared, while the new ₹500 note gradually became the dominant denomination by value.

But the shift was not only about demonetisation. The withdrawal of the ₹2,000 note, the surge in precautionary cash holdings during the COVID-19 pandemic and the growth of digital payments also changed the mix of notes circulating in the economy.

THE ₹500 RISE

In March 2016, ₹500 notes accounted for 47.8% of the total value of banknotes in circulation.

By Aug. 14, 2026, the value of ₹500 notes had risen to ₹36.33 lakh crore, accounting for about 85.5% of the total value of banknotes in circulation.

The increase was particularly significant after demonetisation, as the ₹500 note became one of the main high-value denominations in circulation.

But this does not mean people used ₹500 notes more often. The RBI data measures the value of notes in circulation — the stock of currency — rather than how frequently each denomination was used for transactions.

THE ₹2,000 NOTE

What happened to the ₹2,000 note?

The ₹2,000 note was introduced after demonetisation and quickly became a significant part of the currency stock.

Its value in circulation stood at ₹6.57 lakh crore in March 2017.

The RBI stopped printing ₹2,000 notes in 2018-19. In May 2023, it announced the withdrawal of ₹2,000 notes from circulation, while retaining their status as legal tender.

The value of ₹2,000 notes in circulation subsequently fell to ₹5,285 crore by Aug. 14, 2026.

As the ₹2,000 note declined, the ₹500 note accounted for an increasing share of the value of banknotes in circulation.

THE SHIFT IN CASH

Cash did not disappear. Its composition changed.

The change in denominations also coincided with a shift in how Indians made smaller-value payments.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the RBI said the rise in currency demand was primarily precautionary, with households and businesses holding more cash.

At the same time, the RBI reported that the share of low-denomination notes declined partly because small-value payments were increasingly being substituted by UPI and mobile wallets.

This meant that while digital payments reduced the need for cash for some transactions, the value of physical currency remained concentrated in higher denominations.

A decade of change in India’s cash mix

India’s currency system in 2026 looks different from the one that existed before demonetisation.

The ₹1,000 note is no longer in circulation. The ₹2,000 note has largely returned to the RBI and remains legal tender. Meanwhile, the ₹500 note has become the dominant denomination by value.

The data therefore shows a change in what India's cash stock is made of, rather than simply whether Indians are using more or less cash.
